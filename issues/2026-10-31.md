## How to work with agents now

### [How I work with Agents (as of October 2026)](https://x.com/thorstenball/status/2114386050638336082)
**Thorsten Ball (@thorstenball)** · *X Article (field notes)*

Thorsten Ball (Amp) wrote down the advice he had been sending friends in DMs. Use the latest models: "If a model is wrong 10% of the time, you don't know which 10%." Prompt the *what*, not the *how*. Make the whole SDLC legible to the agent: production logs, deploys, rollouts, feature flags, bug reports. Eval your AGENTS.md files and skills with "five blind agents, control vs. treatment." Ask for "irrefutable proof" (walkthroughs, demo videos, before/after numbers) and put "this is not evidence" in AGENTS.md. Let agents wait on you, not the reverse. Scheduled wake-ups ("Check the logs in 2hrs") are a "killer feature." Stop hand-managing context, and review only high-blast-radius code: fewer than five central files, plus database migrations.

**Why it matters:** This reads like a product roadmap for your coding agent dressed up as advice: SDLC tools, heartbeats, threads as company memory, evals for agent knowledge. And "control vs. treatment" for AGENTS.md is a WebLab experiment with a different name.

### [Why are you still using branches?](https://x.com/thorstenball/status/2115852581364801911)
**Thorsten Ball (@thorstenball)** · *long post (opinion)*

Thorsten follows his anti-PR post with a case for trunk-based development: small commits straight to main, no long-lived branches. He says someone told him they ran it in a 400-engineer org, and that the Ruby project works this way. The old objection was the "meta programming" it took: splitting a migration, adding a feature flag, keeping two configurations alive for a day. His point is that agents are now good at exactly those chores, so "it's now 10x easier to just work on main." He admits trunk-based development is partly "a ruse" to get you to rethink how you build software.

**Why it matters:** For your product pitch, "split this into five flag-guarded commits and watch each one roll out" is a stronger story than "types faster." It's also the kind of discipline a big Amazon codebase already has, so you can test the claim at work.

### [Ship the logging first, then change the feature](https://x.com/AmpCode/status/2115428375766482969)
**Amp (@AmpCode)** · *video clip*

Amp shares a clip of Quinn Slack (@sqs): before he changes a feature, he has the agent add logging, ships only that, and lets it run for an hour or a day. "If I had to wait for another person to go and review that PR, that would slow me down." It quotes Amp's earlier post on how the team works: 21 people, one meeting a week, no pull requests, no mandatory code review, and "a hundred releases a day."

**Why it matters:** Observe-before-you-change costs almost nothing when an agent writes the logging. You could start doing it Monday whether or not you ever give up code review.

### [Yaron Minsky on making your codebase better for agents](https://x.com/poteto/status/2114626630429913383)
**lauren (@poteto)** · *conference talk (YouTube) + summary thread*

lauren (@poteto) summarizes a [talk by Yaron Minsky](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rUYP4C29yCw) of Jane Street. "Agents thrive on feedback," and types are the fastest kind. "Make illegal states unrepresentable" is his catchphrase. Jane Street uses property tests, fuzzing, full-system deterministic simulation, and above all *expect tests*: you print output, capture it in the test, and review the diff when behavior changes. In the talk he says agents "super want to win the video game" but are bad at keeping a codebase simple, so review matters more, not less. On formal methods: seL4 is about 8,700 lines of C, took about 25 person-years, and needed 23 lines of proof per line of code. He says agents may change that math, and that Jane Street wants to start a formal methods team.

**Why it matters:** Every point here is a way to give an agent a tighter feedback loop, which is your whole product. He also mentions Verus, a prover that works well with Rust because ownership removes most sharing.

### [Things to use AI for when working on UI](https://x.com/emilkowalski/status/2115531147853607214)
**Emil Kowalski (@emilkowalski)** · *tips list*

Emil Kowalski's list: have it try to break what you built, generate state machines for different scenarios (success, error, user roles), take before/after screenshots, ask you lots of questions to test the idea, make a few variants of a UI piece to compare, and set up loops that keep checking quality.

**Why it matters:** "Before/after screenshots" is the UI version of Thorsten's "ask for proof." Both are cheap defaults to bake into your agent.

## Harnesses, runtimes, and the agent forge

### [Infinity: the design doc behind 75,000 agents in 8 GB](https://x.com/Shaan_GS/status/2114786631274447288)
**Shaan (@Shaan_GS)** · *AWS Open Source blog post*

Shaan's reply links the [AWS Open Source blog post](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/opensource/introducing-infinity-a-runtime-for-extreme-agent-concurrency/) by Shadaj Laddad (Oct 23). It treats an agent like a stackless coroutine. Each slice loads the history, runs one completion, saves it, and dispatches at most one tool call. Tools return right away and post their results to the agent's input queue later. Idle agents cost about 100 KB each. The reference deployment uses SQS FIFO (one `MessageGroupId` per thread), one Lambda per delivery, Aurora DSQL for histories, DynamoDB for dedup, and EventBridge Scheduler for `sleep`. Events that arrive mid-wait show up as *synthetic tool calls* named `receive_event__injected`. It's Apache 2.0, part of the Hydro project with UC Berkeley, and labeled an early research release.

**Why it matters:** Last issue had the launch post. This is the full write-up, on AWS services you know well. The synthetic tool call is the idea to borrow: it lets you tell a model "CI just finished" without pretending the user said it.

### [Durable code mode in Pi, crash-tested](https://x.com/badlogicgames/status/2116082498115657878)
**Mario Zechner (@badlogicgames)** · *quote-post of an X Article (also a blog post)*

Mario Zechner (@badlogicgames) recommends Manoj's write-up, also posted on [StackToHeap](https://stacktoheap.com/blog/2026/10/26/durable-code-mode-in-pi/). In code mode, the model writes one script that calls the tools, and only the script's output goes back to the model. In his small test, the median tool output that reached the model in Pi fell from 26,820 bytes to 4,374, and Pi's logged cost fell from $0.037 to $0.021. Pi Durable resumes a crashed script without re-running finished calls (0 of 18 re-issued). But in the "changed data" trial the result was "Wrong, no error."

**Why it matters:** That last row is the honest part. Durable replay can quietly return a wrong answer when the data changed underneath it, so you need a plan for that before you sell "crash-proof."

### [Entire: a Git forge for agent-human collaboration](https://x.com/ashtom/status/2114763996691562540)
**Thomas Dohmke (@ashtom)** · *product launch thread*

Thomas Dohmke's company ships [Entire](https://entire.com/). The thread covers four pieces. Git hosting replicates across regions or mirrors from GitHub. "Continuous Memory" says session logs are "the second most important artifact in software development" and belong in the repo, and it claims up to 71% fewer tokens. Trails replaces the PR with a loop where evals feed the next round of agent work, with CI from Buildkite and Depot. It works with Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Pi, Copilot, and AWS Kiro. The site's own benchmark puts Entire at 418 pushes per second and GitHub at 4.7. Those are vendor numbers.

**Why it matters:** Agent session logs stored in the repo next to the code is a position your product will have to take, for or against. Note that Trails pairs with the anti-PR posts above.

### [skill-diet: put your agent skills on a diet](https://x.com/nicopreme/status/2114975730471997613)
**Nico Bailon (@nicopreme)** · *open-source agent skill (GitHub)*

Nico Bailon's [skill-diet](https://github.com/nicobailon/skill-diet) trims what the model already knows, moves rarely needed detail into files it reads only when needed, and fixes commands that no longer match the tool. Then it runs the same tasks with the old skill, the new skill, and no skill, and keeps the new one only if it does at least as well. He reports 70–80% fewer tokens with the same results. The README ranks command formats by token cost: usage lines 1×, markdown table 1.5×, JSON Schema 2.8×, OpenAPI 4.7×.

**Why it matters:** That format table applies right away to how your agent describes its own tools. The "keep it only if it wins" gate is an A/B test with a rollback rule.

### [/statechart: draw your state machines, find real bugs](https://x.com/poteto/status/2115880102601101414)
**lauren (@poteto)** · *agent skill release (pstack)*

lauren (@poteto) says the `/statechart` skill in pstack v0.15.36 is stable. It pairs with `/create-formal-verification-skill` to model state machines and distributed systems and find bugs. She says it includes "a mini tlc compiler" that parses, lowers to a small IR, and outputs Mermaid. Charts render with [Pele](https://pele.run/), a zero-dependency Mermaid-to-SVG renderer built for Obsidian that claims to be up to 500× faster than Mermaid.js. Her chart shows two real bugs it found.

**Why it matters:** This is the cheap formal methods Minsky is betting on, packaged as a skill. Try pointing it at your agent's own session state machine.

## Hardware, homelab, and one long read

### [Your smart TV is cheap because you're the product](https://x.com/Tristan0x/status/2114311011561812476)
**Tristan (@Tristan0x)** · *thread (privacy guide)*

Tristan builds on @log4j, who rooted a 2025 Samsung TV and [found](https://x.com/log4j/status/2113762242327892456) that a TV without a mic will use one on a connected soundbar or Bluetooth device. Automatic content recognition (ACR) fingerprints the screen, every 500 ms on Samsung and every 10 ms on LG, according to an [arXiv study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06203), and that includes HDMI inputs. His fix: turn ACR off (Samsung "Viewing Information Services," LG "Live Plus," Vizio "Viewing Data"), put smart devices on a guest/IoT network, run NextDNS (or Pi-hole) with the OISD blocklist, and block outbound port 53. Per Vizio's Q3 2024 results, it lost $6.7M on TVs and made $115.8M gross profit from ads and viewing data.

**Why it matters:** You already run Tailscale and Caddy, so Pi-hole plus an IoT VLAN is a Saturday afternoon. Do it before the Denon upgrade puts another box with a network port on the shelf.

### [Computer chips are the most magnificent things we've ever made](https://x.com/thorstenball/status/2115346307170607261)
**Thorsten Ball (@thorstenball)** · *magazine feature (The New Yorker)*

Thorsten recommends Gideon Lewis-Kraus's [New Yorker feature](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/10/26/computer-chips-are-the-most-magnificent-things-weve-ever-made) on TSMC and ASML. If you blew a Zeiss EUV mirror up to the size of the planet, "the highest mountain would have the elevation of a face-down playing card." Inside the machine, tin droplets are hit by lasers sixty thousand times a second, making plasma fifty times hotter than the surface of the sun. A machine ships in a fleet of seven 747s, ASML makes about fifty a year, and each costs up to four hundred million dollars. A wafer is "the diameter of a vinyl record."

**Why it matters:** It's the best long read of the week for a vintage computing person. The Connecticut ASML shop "hires the sorts of people who fix their own motorcycles," and that describes your bench too.
